Embedding diagnostics Research case study / 2026

Can you trust
an embedding?

Models turn images and captions into vectors: lists of numbers used for classification and search. This study asks what the geometry of those vectors tells us about the tasks they can still perform.

Read the study ↓
Problem
A representation can look unusual geometrically while retaining useful information. It can also look broadly distributed while search results lose their relevance.
Approach
Controlled training, fixed-encoder stress tests, dimensional compression, numerical precision checks, and paired image–caption retrieval.
Finding
Distribution, recoverable labels, neighbour identity, and caption coverage are different properties. Evaluate the property the application actually needs.
01 / Motivation

Search needs useful neighbours.

Imagine looking through a large collection of road images for situations similar to a rainy night on a city street. An embedding-based system ranks images by the similarity of their vectors.

The problem

Checking every result requires labels or human judgement. Geometric measurements are cheaper: they describe vector spread, direction, and dimensional structure without labels. But a warning from geometry does not tell us which task has stopped working.

The research question

Can label-free measurements tell us whether an embedding still supports classification and useful search?

The study deliberately separates what the vectors look like from what a classifier or retrieval system can do with them. Its contribution is an instrument comparison with explicit limitations, rather than a new embedding model or a universal quality score.

02 / Approach

Change the representation. Keep the question fixed.

Two complementary designs test the measurements: train controlled image models, then alter cached outputs from one pretrained model. They share the diagnostic questions, rather than a directly comparable score scale.

Controlled training

Seven variants. One training loop.

A Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) predicts hidden image-region representations from visible regions. The encoder turns images into vectors; the predictor performs the hidden-region prediction. [1]

Change stop-gradient, a slowly updated target encoder, distribution regularization, or a projector. All seven conditions use the same training loop. Five matched seeds per condition give 35 completed runs, measured at the final 4,000-step checkpoint.

Fixed pretrained encoder

One model. Controlled vector changes.

EmbeddingGemma 2 maps images and text into a common 768-dimensional space. The study pins one revision, extracts and caches its outputs, and evaluates those vectors without retraining the encoder. [6]

Test imposed contraction, offsets, direction restriction and noise; compare compression; perturb numerical storage and scoring; then evaluate original image–caption pairs. These post-hoc changes do not establish what naturally occurs during training.

Images / captions→Pinned encoder→Cached vectors→Controlled change→Geometry + task checks

Inside the training experiment

Stop-gradient
Prevents the target side of a prediction from being updated through that prediction’s gradient.
EMA target
A separate target encoder updated by an exponential moving average, a slowly changing copy of the main encoder.
SIGReg
Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization: a published loss that encourages the embedding distribution to resemble a standard Gaussian. [2]
Projector
A temporary neural-network layer between the encoder and SIGReg. Evaluation reads the encoder, not this layer.

Data / Evaluation

Two datasets. Different definitions of similarity.

Driving attributes test coarse visual context. Recorded image–caption pairs test whether the corresponding image or captions are recovered. Neither is a complete definition of semantic relevance.

Driving context

BDD100K

Berkeley DeepDrive contains 100,000 driving videos; this project uses annotated still images. Weather, scene type, and time of day provide three label-based checks on the embeddings. [3]

The pretrained analysis uses a BDD100K-derived driving-scene subset with 2,000 training images and 983 validation images. Training images fit the classifier and PCA basis; validation images measure classification and retrieval.

Recorded correspondence

COCO image–caption pairs

COCO contains everyday-scene photographs with human annotations. This study samples 1,000 original-release validation image groups, five captions each, to test image-to-text and text-to-image retrieval. [8]

For a caption query, its recorded parent image is the positive. For an image query, its five recorded captions are positives. Other semantically valid matches may exist without being annotated.

No labels

Geometry

Variance: centered spread. Cosine: directional agreement. RankMe: raw singular-spectrum count. Participation ratio: centered covariance-spectrum count. Their definitions and blind spots differ. [4] Mathematical explanation ↓

Labels used

Classification

Freeze the encoder and fit a simple classifier, a linear probe. Accuracy counts correct predictions. Balanced accuracy averages class recall over the frozen eligible classes, so common classes do not dominate it.

Labels used

Search

Rank candidates by cosine similarity. BDD P@10 counts neighbours sharing the query’s attribute. COCO Hit@10 asks whether any recorded positive is found; caption recall@10 counts the fraction of all five captions recovered.

Training probes use raw embeddings with validation-selected regularization. Pretrained BDD probes standardize features. The studies also use different samples and output normalization: their absolute scores and variance scales are not an encoder benchmark. 06 / Neighbour stability

Small score errors can change who makes the top ten.

Store cached vectors with fewer bits or change similarity arithmetic, then repeat exact search. FP16 uses 16-bit floating point; INT8 uses 8-bit integer quantization. Neither condition changes the encoder’s inference precision.

Read across each row: how many queries returned different neighbours, and how much their similarity scores changed. Compare storage changes against the native reference; compare offset + FP16 against the common-offset reference. Error uses a logarithmic scale.

__NUMERICS_FIGURES__

Error magnitude alone does not explain neighbour stability. Under the common-offset stress, FP16 changes 195 top-10 sets despite a smaller maximum score error than native FP16, which changes five.

Native FP16 changes all lie in the smallest reference-margin bin. Reconstruction-based sufficient conditions cover 576 native FP16-gallery queries with no observed exception, but cover no INT8 or stressed queries. These are empirical checks of sufficient conditions, not rigorous floating-point certification.

Of the 195 stressed identity changes, weather P@10 stays unchanged for 131 queries, scene for 119, and time of day for 149. Coarse attribute agreement can hide neighbour turnover; it does not establish unchanged object layout, instance meaning, or safety relevance.

08 / Conclusions

Measure the property you need.

The useful outcome is a clearer evaluation practice: treat each diagnostic as an instrument with a definition and a scope, then check the task property being claimed.

  1. Read geometry as geometry.

    Spread and spectral counts can reveal contraction or directional concentration. They do not, alone, identify what labels are recoverable or what makes a useful neighbour.

  2. Separate utility endpoints.

    Classification, coarse neighbour agreement, exact neighbour identity, first-rank recovery, and multiple-caption coverage can respond differently to the same change.

  3. Keep the evidence scope attached.

    Training-seed variation, fixed-sample BDD effects, and conditional COCO bootstrap intervals answer different uncertainty questions.

What the study does not establish

Dataset scope

The BDD-derived validation subset contains 983 images. It has no foggy-weather, gas-station, or tunnel examples, so the results do not cover those categories.

Encoder scope

The pretrained results use one encoder, EmbeddingGemma 2. A second-encoder replication with Jina-CLIP-v2 could not be completed because its precision checks failed. The findings therefore have not been replicated across encoders.

Claim scope

The study establishes no universal quality predictor, full LeJEPA reproduction, independent encoder replication, operational search-speed benefit, or deployment certification. Familiar compression behavior is reported with its limitations.

What the interval covers

For the five differences, let d̄ be their mean and sd their sample standard deviation. The interval is d̄ ± 2.776 × sd / √5, using Student’s t distribution with four degrees of freedom. One percentage point means, for example, a change from 90% to 91%.

These intervals describe training-seed variation on one fixed evaluation split. They do not cover a different dataset, image split, or architecture. The five comparisons were declared before these runs; no correction for multiple comparisons is applied. The classifier-fitting settings were selected after training. How are dimensionality measurements calculated?
Technical note / Rank diagnostics

How many directions
carry the representation?

An embedding has 192 coordinates, but these can move together. If every image varies along one line, 192 coordinates describe only one independent direction. These measurements give a smooth count of how broadly the representation uses its directions, rather than counting every tiny nonzero direction equally.

Start with the matrix of image embeddings.

Stack n images as the rows of a matrix Z ∈ ℝn×d, with d = 192 coordinates per image. A spectrum lists the strength of independent, perpendicular directions in that matrix. Both measurements turn a spectrum into relative shares, then ask how evenly those shares are distributed.

01 / Participation ratio

Count directions of variation around the mean.

Subtract the mean embedding μ from each row to obtain X. The covariance matrix is C = XᵀX / (n − 1). Its eigenvalues λᵢ ≥ 0 are the variances along its independent directions. Their sum is total variance.

qi=λi∑jλj PR=(∑iλi)2∑iλi2=1∑iqi2

Why this makes sense: squaring the shares makes a dominant direction weigh heavily. If one direction holds everything, the sum of squared shares is 1 and PR is 1. If k directions each hold 1/k, that sum is 1/k and PR is k. Unequal shares give an intermediate count.

02 / RankMe

Count directions in the raw matrix.

Use Z without subtracting its mean. Singular value decomposition gives strengths σᵢ ≥ 0 for its perpendicular directions. Normalize these strengths into shares pᵢ, then calculate their entropy. [4]

pi=σi∑jσj RankMe=exp(−∑ipilnpi)

Why exponentiate entropy? One direction has entropy 0, so its count is exp(0) = 1. With k equal shares, entropy is ln(k); exponentiating returns k. This puts the measurement in units of directions. Here ln is the natural logarithm and zero shares contribute zero to entropy.

A worked example: two directions, one much stronger.

Illustration only, not experiment data. Take a zero-mean matrix with singular values (3, 1, 0, 0). Because it is already centered, we can isolate the difference in weighting:

  1. Covariance eigenvalues are proportional to squared singular values: (9, 1, 0, 0). Variance shares are (0.9, 0.1, 0, 0), giving PR = 1 / (0.9² + 0.1²) ≈ 1.22.
  2. RankMe uses singular-value shares (0.75, 0.25, 0, 0), giving exp[−0.75 ln(0.75) − 0.25 ln(0.25)] ≈ 1.75.

Both counts lie between one and two, because two directions are present but contribute unequally. They differ because variance squares the singular values, while RankMe uses their unsquared strengths.

What these counts cannot tell you

Centering matters. Adding the same offset vector to every embedding leaves centered covariance and PR unchanged. Raw RankMe can change because that shared mean contributes to the singular-value spectrum.

Magnitude matters separately. Multiplying every embedding by a small positive number leaves both normalized spectra unchanged, while total variance falls by the square of that number. A representation can therefore shrink without either count falling. Neither count identifies which directions encode useful labels; read them alongside variance, classification, and retrieval.

The equations show the ideal nonzero-spectrum case. The implementation adds 10−7 to RankMe’s normalized singular values for numerical stability and returns 1 for an exactly zero spectrum in either measurement. That convention is not evidence of useful information. [5]
